# Song of Songs 2

¹ I am the hyssop of the Sharon, the rose of the valleys. ² Like a rose among the thorns, so is my beloved among the daughters. ³ Like an apple tree among the trees of the forest, so is my beloved among the sons. In his shade I longed and sat down, and his fruit was sweet in my throat. ⁴ Lead me into the house of wine, and let love be raised over me. ⁵ Sustain me with cakes of raisins, refresh me with apples, for I am faint with love. ⁶ His left hand is under my head, and his right hand embraces me. ⁷ I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles and by the hinds of the field, that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases. ⁸ The voice of my beloved! Here he comes, leaping over the mountains, bounding over the hills. ⁹ My beloved is like a gazelle or a young stag. Here he stands behind our wall, looking through the windows, glancing through the lattice. ¹⁰ My beloved speaks and says to me: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away." ¹¹ For behold, the winter has passed, the rain has ceased, it has gone from us. ¹² The blossoms appear in the land, the time of singing has come, and the voice of the turtledove is heard in our land. ¹³ The fig tree has given its fruit, the vines are in blossom, giving off fragrance. Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away. ¹⁴ My dove, my perfect one, is enclosed in the clefts of the rock, hidden in the covert of the cliff. Let me see your face, let me hear your voice, for your voice is sweet, and your face is lovely. ¹⁵ Catch for us the foxes, the little foxes that ruin the vineyards, while our vineyards are in blossom. ¹⁶ My beloved is mine, and I am his; he grazes among the lilies. ¹⁷ Until the day breaks and the shadows flee away, turn, my beloved, be like a gazelle or a young stag on the mountains of Bether.

---

Notes
- v. 1: "Hyssop" is a literal translation of חֲבַצֶּלֶת, a term usually associated with a plant or herb; "hyssop" is the most accurate botanical match.
- v. 7: "Gazelles and hinds of the field" reflects the poetic parallel of צְבָאוֹת and אַיְלוֹת, which are literally "armies" and "rams" but are used here in a metaphorical, naturalistic sense.
- v. 9: "Young stag" is used for עֹפֶר, a term often rendered "fawn" in other translations, to better reflect the poetic imagery and natural setting.
