# Ecclesiastes 2

¹ I said in my heart, "Come now, let me test pleasure and see what is good, and behold, it too is folly." ² I said about laughter, "It is madness," and about joy, "What does it achieve?" ³ I devoted my heart to seeking how to provoke my flesh with wine, while my heart was guided by wisdom, and how to grasp folly until I might see what is good for the children of humanity to do under the heavens during the number of their days. ⁴ I enlarged my works. I built myself houses and planted for myself vineyards. ⁵ I made for myself gardens and orchards, planting in them every kind of fruit tree. ⁶ I made for myself pools of water to water with them a growing forest of trees. ⁷ I acquired slaves and female servants, and I had many sons of the household. I also had a great number of cattle and sheep, more than all who were before me in Jerusalem. ⁸ I collected for myself silver and gold, and the treasure of kings and provinces. I made for myself singers and players of instruments, and the delights of human beings I enjoyed—concubines and wives. ⁹ And I became great and added to all that was before me in Jerusalem. My wisdom stood firm with me. ¹⁰ And every thing my eyes desired I did not withhold from them. I did not withhold my heart from any joy, because my heart was joyful in all my labor, and this was my reward from all my labor. ¹¹ And I turned my gaze to all my works that my hands had done and to the labor I had toiled to do, and behold, all was folly and a striving after wind, and there was no profit under the sun. ¹² And I turned to see wisdom and madness and folly. What will the man do who comes after the king, who has already done all this? ¹³ And I saw that wisdom has an advantage over folly, just as light has an advantage over darkness. ¹⁴ The wise has eyes in their head, but the fool walks in darkness. And I also know that the same fate will befall them all. ¹⁵ And I said in my heart, "What happens to the fool will also happen to me. Then why have I been wise?" And I said in my heart, "This too is folly." ¹⁶ For there is no memory of the wise and the fool forever, once the days to come have passed. All will be forgotten. And how will the wise die like the fool? ¹⁷ And I hated life, because the work done under the sun was evil to me, for all is folly and a striving after wind. ¹⁸ And I hated all my labor that I had labored under the sun, because I must leave it to the man who will come after me. ¹⁹ And who knows if the wise will be the one to inherit, or the fool? Yet the one who toiled and gained wisdom under the sun—this too is folly. ²⁰ And I dedicated my heart to be vexed over all the labor I had labored under the sun. ²¹ For there is a man who labors in wisdom and understanding and skill, and to another man who did not labor in them, they give it as his portion. This too is folly and great evil. ²² For what benefit does a man have from all his labor and the unrest of his heart with which he labors under the sun? ²³ For all his days are pain and vexation, his work is troubling to him, and even at night his heart does not rest. This too is folly. ²⁴ There is no good for a human except to eat and drink and see good in their labor. This I have seen to be from the hand of God. ²⁵ For who can eat or who can make the heart feel joy besides me? ²⁶ For to a man whose face is good, God has given wisdom and knowledge and joy, but to the sinner He has given the business of gathering and collecting to give to the one whose face is good from the hand of God. This too is folly and a striving after wind.

Notes
- v. 2: "What does it achieve?" — The Hebrew uses "mah zeh oseh," a rhetorical question implying futility.
- v. 12: "What will the man do who comes after the king..." — Reflects the idea of legacy and the futility of lasting achievement.
- v. 24: "to see good in their labor" — The phrase "her'eh et nafsho tov" is rendered to maintain clarity and natural English.
- v. 25: "make the heart feel joy" — "Yachush chush" is a poetic expression of emotional satisfaction, translated to reflect its nuance.
- v. 26: "the business of gathering and collecting" — The Hebrew uses "inyan la'asot v'liknot," indicating a burdensome task, not a virtuous one.
