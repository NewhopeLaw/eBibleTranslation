# Acts 25

¹ Festus had just taken up the governorship, and after three days he went up to Jerusalem from Caesarea, ² and the high priests and the leading Jews presented Paul to him, and they began to ask him ³ for a favor concerning him, that he might be sent to Jerusalem, setting a trap to kill him along the way. ⁴ But Festus replied that Paul should be kept in Caesarea, and said that he himself was about to leave quickly; ⁵ Let those among you, he said, be able to come down if there is anything unusual about the man to accuse him. ⁶ When he had spent no more than eight or ten days among them, he went down to Caesarea, and the next day he sat on the judgment seat and ordered Paul to be brought. ⁷ When he had arrived, the men from Jerusalem who had come down stood before him, bringing many and heavy accusations which they could not prove, ⁸ while Paul, defending himself, said, I have done nothing against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar. ⁹ Festus, wanting to do a favor to the Jews, replied to Paul, Do you want to go up to Jerusalem and be judged there under me? ¹⁰ Paul said, I am standing on Caesar's tribunal, where I should be judged. I have done no wrong to the Jews, as you know better. ¹¹ If I have done anything wrong and am guilty of death, I do not refuse to die; if there is nothing in these things that they accuse me of, then no one can give them a favor. I appeal to Caesar. ¹² Then Festus, speaking with the council, replied, You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go. ¹³ After some days had passed, King Agrippa and Bernice arrived at Caesarea to greet Festus. ¹⁴ As they were spending more days there, Festus addressed the king, laying out the case against Paul, saying, There is a certain man, whom Felix left in prison, ¹⁵ concerning whom, when I went to Jerusalem, the high priests and the elders of the Jews presented themselves to me, asking for his condemnation. ¹⁶ I replied to them that it is not the custom of the Romans to give a man over without the accused having the opportunity to face his accusers and have a place to defend himself regarding the charge. ¹⁷ So I did not delay here, but the next day I sat on the judgment seat and ordered the man to be brought. ¹⁸ When the accusers stood, they brought no charges of the evil things I expected, ¹⁹ but they had certain matters of their own religious fears and concerning some Jesus who had died, whom Paul claimed was alive. ²⁰ I was at a loss about their investigation, so I said whether he wanted to go to Jerusalem to be judged there. ²¹ But when Paul appealed to be held for Caesar's examination, I ordered him to be kept until I send him to Caesar. ²² Agrippa said to Festus, I, too, would like to hear this man. Tomorrow, he said, you will hear him. ²³ So the next day, when Agrippa and Bernice had come in with great ceremony and entered the hearing room, and when the chiliarch and the prominent men of the city had also come in, and Festus gave the order, Paul was brought in. ²⁴ And Festus said, Agrippa, King, and all you who are with us, look at this man about whom all the Jewish people met with me in Jerusalem and here, shouting that he should no longer be allowed to live. ²⁵ But I found nothing in him deserving death, and because he appealed to Caesar, I decided to send him. ²⁶ I have no definite information to write to the emperor regarding this matter, and so I have brought him to you, especially to you, King Agrippa, so that after the inquiry I may have something to write; ²⁷ for it seems unreasonable to me to send a prisoner without also indicating the charges against him.

---

Notes
- v. 10: "I have done no wrong to the Jews, as you know better" — the Greek uses *γνωρίζειν* ('know') in a legal sense, indicating Festus is a better judge of the facts.
- v. 20: "I was at a loss about their investigation" — the Greek *διαπορῶ* is a rare form of *διαπορία* ('perplexity'), indicating confusion or a dead end in the inquiry.
- v. 27: "it seems unreasonable to me" — the Greek *ἄλογον γάρ μοι δοκεῖ* uses *ἄλογον* ('unreasonable') in a legal context, not as 'illogical' in the modern sense, but as a matter of administrative prudence.
