# Hebrews 5

¹ Every high priest is taken from among people and is appointed to serve on their behalf before God, to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins, ² being able to sympathize with those who are ignorant and who go astray, since he himself is also surrounded by weakness, ³ and because of this, he must offer sacrifices for his own sins as well as for those of the people. ⁴ No one takes the honor for himself, but he is called by God, just as Aaron was. ⁵ Likewise, Christ did not glorify himself by becoming a high priest, but the one who spoke to him, "You are my Son, today I have begotten you," ⁶ and also in another place it is said of him, "You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek," ⁷ who, in the days of his flesh, offered up prayers and supplications to the one who was able to save him from death, with loud cries and tears, and was heard because of his devotion, ⁸ although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered, ⁹ and having been perfected, he became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him, ¹⁰ having been called by God as a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek. ¹¹ Regarding him, we have much to say, and it is hard to explain, because you have become sluggish in hearing. ¹² For although you should be teachers, you need someone to teach you again the basic elements of the words of God, and you have become ones needing milk, not solid food. ¹³ For anyone who partakes of milk is without experience of the word of righteousness, since he is still a child. ¹⁴ But solid food is for the mature, for those whose senses have been trained through practice to discern between good and evil.

---

Notes  
- v. 5: "You are my Son, today I have begotten you" — the Greek uses "σήμερον," translated as "today," which is included to maintain the context of divine declaration and timing.  
- v. 10: "called by God as a high priest" — the phrase "ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ" is rendered as "by God" to clarify the divine appointment.  
- v. 12: "need someone to teach you again" — the Greek uses "πάλιν χρείαν ἔχετε τοῦ διδάσκειν ὑμᾶς," which is naturally rendered as "need someone to teach you again" to reflect the idea of regression or needing to return to foundational instruction.
