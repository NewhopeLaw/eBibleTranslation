# Genesis 50

¹ Joseph fell on his father’s face and wept over him, then kissed him. ² Joseph gave orders to his attendants, the physicians, to embalm his father, and the physicians embalmed Israel. ³ They spent forty days with him, for such is the full time required for embalming. And the people of Egypt mourned for him seventy days. ⁴ When the days of mourning had passed, Joseph spoke to Pharaoh’s household, saying, “If I now find favor in your eyes, please speak in Pharaoh’s ears to this effect: ⁵ My father made me swear, saying, ‘I am going to die in the grave that I dug for myself in the land of Canaan; there I will be buried.’ Now I must go up and bury my father, and I will return.” ⁶ Pharaoh said, “Go up and bury your father, as he made you swear.” ⁷ So Joseph went up to bury his father, and with him went all Pharaoh’s officials, the elders of his court, and all the elders of Egypt. ⁸ In addition, the whole house of Joseph and his brothers and his father’s household, leaving behind only their little ones and their flocks and herds, went up to Canaan. ⁹ Joseph also took along chariots and horsemen, and a very large company, so that the company was heavy with mourning. ¹⁰ When they arrived at the threshing floor of Atad, which is across the Jordan, they mourned there with a great and very heavy lamentation. Joseph observed a mourning period of seven days for his father. ¹¹ The people living in the land, the Canaanites, saw the mourning at the threshing floor of Atad and said, “This is a heavy mourning for Egypt.” That is why the name of the place was called Abel-Mizraim, which is across the Jordan. ¹² Joseph did for his father as his father had commanded. ¹³ Joseph and his brothers brought him up to the land of Canaan and buried him in the cave in the field of Machpelah, which Abraham had bought as a possession for a burial site from Ephron the Hittite, on the face of Mamre. ¹⁴ Joseph returned to Egypt, he and his brothers and all who had gone up with him to bury his father, after they had buried him. ¹⁵ When Joseph’s brothers saw that their father was dead, they said, “What if Joseph bears a grudge against us and pays us back in full for all the wrong we did to him?” ¹⁶ So they sent a message to Joseph, saying, “Your father gave this command before he died: ¹⁷ This is what you must say to Joseph, ‘Please forgive, we beg you, the transgression of your brothers and their sin, for indeed they treated you badly. Now, please forgive the transgression of the servants of the God of your father.’ Joseph wept when they spoke to him.” ¹⁸ Then his brothers also went and fell before him, saying, “Here we are, your servants.” ¹⁹ Joseph said to them, “Do not fear, for am I in the place of God? ²⁰ You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good, to bring about this present result, to preserve many people alive.” ²¹ And Joseph said to them, “Do not fear; I will provide for you and your children.” So he comforted them and spoke to their hearts. ²² Joseph remained in Egypt, along with his father’s household. Joseph lived a hundred and ten years. ²³ Joseph saw Ephraim’s children to the third generation, and the children of Manasseh’s son Machir were also born on Joseph’s knees. ²⁴ Joseph said to his brothers, “I am about to die, but God will surely visit you and bring you up from this land to the land of which He swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.” ²⁵ Joseph made the Israelites swear, saying, “God will surely visit you, and you must carry my bones up from this place.” ²⁶ Joseph died at the age of a hundred and ten years. They embalmed him and placed him in a coffin in Egypt.

---

Notes
- v. 20: "God intended it for good" reflects the Hebrew phrase "God turned it for good" (God had a different intention than their own). The nuance of divine purpose is preserved.
- v. 23: "Ephraim’s children to the third generation" and "the children of Manasseh’s son Machir were also born on Joseph’s knees" are translated to preserve the poetic parallelism and the symbolic gesture of blessing.
- v. 24–25: The phrase "God will surely visit you" is a standard rendering of פָּקֹד יִפְקֹד, which denotes a divine reckoning or action.
